# Most Languages Reinvented What Perl 6 Designed

Perl 6 specified most of its signature features between Larry Wall's July 2000 announcement and Apocalypse 12 in April 2004, often years before C#, Swift, Kotlin, Rust, Julia or modern Python shipped look-alikes, yet outside the Perl family the adopting languages' own design records document **only five borrowings, and all but one were partial**: Swift 4 took Perl 6's rule that the closing delimiter's indentation sets a multi-line string's margin — proposed on swift-evolution in 2016 as Perl 6's rule by two future co-authors of SE-0168 and accepted in April 2017 without credit, the one Perl 6 design decision to ship intact in a major non-Perl language; Ruby took the `->` lambda arrow (committed July 2005, shipped in 1.9.0 in December 2007) but not pointy-block semantics; PHP 8.3 aligned string increment with Raku's behaviour (RFC accepted 25–0 in July 2023); Apple's Swift string lead planned in 2018 to take whitespace-insensitive patterns, interpolation and ratcheting from Perl 6, then shipped PCRE-style literals in Swift 5.7 (2022); and JavaScript's Joose reached the Perl 6 object model only through Perl 5's Moose. **Perl 5 is the one real importer** — `//`, `say`, `state`, given/when, `~~`, `\h`/`\v`, two backtracking verbs, Moose, Regexp::Grammars and the design of the 5.38 `class` feature all trace to Perl 6, and `use feature` itself began in 2002 as a `use perl6ish` pragma for them — and its smartmatch, copied from a specification that kept moving, drew an apology from Larry Wall two weeks after 5.10.0 shipped, was declared a failed experiment in 2023 and survives only behind feature flags. Nearly every other overlap examined, across roughly thirty languages, is either **independent reinvention** (indentation-stripping heredocs outside Swift and Perl 5, half-open ranges, implicit lambda parameters, keyword-only arguments, CLIs generated from signatures, optional typing) or **descent from an ancestor Perl 6 shared** (the 2003 Traits paper, CLOS and Dylan, APL and NumPy, CLU and Icon generators, Unix pipes, Ada subtypes, Unicode UAX #29, C# async and Rx). Several famous Perl 6 features were themselves imports: junctions and switch began as Damian Conway's Perl 5 modules in August 2000, roles came from the Traits paper, and Supplies came from Rx. The cause is timing and citation habit: Perl 6 was first on paper but had no stable release until December 2015, so designers cited the languages that shipped — Python, C#, Haskell, Perl 5 — and in other communities' archives Perl 6 appears more often as a cautionary tale about breaking compatibility than as a source, often in the words of those communities' own leaders. A November 2026 full-text search of the Swift, Rust, Kotlin, C#, Scala and Dart proposal archives and the JEPs found Raku only as prior art — most visibly in C#'s chained-comparison proposal, approved in April 2026 — and never as the stated source of a shipped feature. A second pass over design forums, mailing lists, twenty more design repositories and seventeen issue trackers found the Swift heredoc credit in the swift-evolution thread rather than in the proposal, core-team members in Swift, Rust, Go and D arguing from Perl 6 with no further shipped result, and two transmissions below the language level, in a Python regex library and a Rust grammar tool. **Raku's most distinctive ideas — junctions, hyper-operators, Whatever-currying, NFG synthetic graphemes, grammars as classes and slangs — have no documented adopter outside the Perl family**, and Swift, F# and Julia core members have rejected junctions or NFG in writing.

## Perl 6 led on paper by years but shipped a decade late

Any priority claim depends on which of three Perl 6 dates one uses. The proposal layer is the **361 RFCs** submitted between 1 August and late September 2000, after Larry Wall announced the redesign at the State of the Onion on 19 July 2000 ([RFC index](https://web.archive.org/web/20191117000414/https://raku.org/archive/rfc/); [Wikipedia: Raku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raku_(programming_language))). The specification layer is Wall's Apocalypses, Damian Conway's Exegeses and the Synopses; only A1–A6 (April 2001 – March 2003) and A12 (16 April 2004) were ever written, and the Synopses began with S05 in June 2002 and were maintained until about 2015 ([A12](https://www.perl.com/pub/2004/04/16/a12.html/); [S05](https://github.com/Raku/old-design-docs/blob/master/S05-regex.pod)). The implementation layer starts with Pugs, Audrey Tang's Haskell interpreter, first released on **7 February 2005**: it ran junctions and chained comparisons in its first release and had multis, signatures, rules via PGE, reduce, hyper-operators and gather/take by late May ([Pugs ChangeLog](https://github.com/audreyt/pugs/blob/master/ChangeLog)). Rakudo was named in January 2008, released independently of Parrot from February 2009, shipped Rakudo Star on **29 July 2010** and the first stable language version, **6.c, on 25 December 2015**, followed by 6.d in November 2018 ([Michaud](https://raku.github.io/CCR/Remaster/Patrick%20Michaud/The-compiler-formerly-known-as-raku.html); [TPF: Rakudo Star](https://news.perlfoundation.org/post/rakudo_star_-_a_useful_usable); [Rakudo 2015.12](https://github.com/rakudo/rakudo/blob/main/docs/announce/2015.12.md); [Rakudo 2018.11](https://github.com/rakudo/rakudo/blob/main/docs/announce/2018.11.md)). As of Rakudo 2026.09 (28 September 2026) RakuAST is the default frontend, but 6.e is still a preview ([Rakudo 2026.09](https://github.com/rakudo/rakudo/blob/main/docs/announce/2026.09.md)).

Two caveats matter for anyone weighing priority claims. First, Apocalypse decisions were often reversed: A3 (October 2001) defined binary `~~` as logical XOR and A4 (January 2002) spelled smartmatch `=~`; Larry Wall floated `~~` for smartmatch on perl6-language on 24 October 2002, among several spellings, once `~` had become string concatenation, the list's weekly summary counted it as settled on 1 November, and A6 (March 2003) was the first Apocalypse to use it ([A3](https://www.perl.com/pub/2001/10/02/apocalypse3.html/); [A4](https://www.perl.com/pub/2002/01/15/apo4.html/); [msg11632](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/2002/10/msg11632.html); [msg12116](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/2002/11/msg12116.html); [A6](https://www.perl.com/pub/2003/03/07/apocalypse6.html/)). The earliest mention in the design record — an Apocalypse, or the perl6-language decision that preceded it — is the fair date for when an idea was first specified, but the final semantics usually come from later Synopsis edits. Second, a block of features commonly treated as Perl 6 originals arrived late enough to be contemporaries of their look-alikes elsewhere, or later than them:

Spec dates before April 2006 no longer rest on the Apocalypses and Exegeses alone. `Raku/old-design-docs` begins with an April 2006 mirror and the canonical November 2008 import, but the Pugs svn mirror `Raku/mu` preserves the complete svn.perl.org Synopsis history — 948 commits from S05 version 1 (July 2002) to the move into Pugs on 26 November 2008, with revisions before February 2005 dated by their Last Modified headers — and from 23 February 2006 every Synopsis commit was also mailed, with its diff, to perl6-language ([Raku/mu](https://github.com/Raku/mu); [first commit](https://github.com/Raku/mu/commit/ddda1b02a); [perl6-language](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/)). That record pins down several famous names and operators, most of them later than their reputation: the word junction was coined on the list on 30–31 October 2002, when Wall asked for a friendlier name than quantum superposition and took one from a list of candidates Conway offered ([msg11842](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/2002/10/msg11842.html); [msg11927](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/2002/10/msg11927.html); [msg12007](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/2002/10/msg12007.html)); `»op«` replaced A3's `^op` on 1 November 2002 ([msg12138](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/2002/11/msg12138.html)); the cross metaoperator `X` entered S03 on 12 September 2006 and zip became `Z` on 21 February 2007 ([088e2dc](https://github.com/Raku/mu/commit/088e2dcaee37c436ff3439c4ce5e01dce6a9df27); [c761027](https://github.com/Raku/mu/commit/c761027ac84540afeb103e20cc02e20a9f42e1fa)); and the sequence operator `...`, with its arithmetic and geometric deduction, arrived in a single S03 revision on 5 October 2008 ([18d3149](https://github.com/Raku/mu/commit/18d31499442176bc34d608458348399b250a35c8)). "≤" in the tables marks a date by which a feature was already present.

The verdicts use a fixed scale. **Borrowed (documented)** means the adopter's own record — a PEP, RFC, KEEP, Swift Evolution text, commit message, or the designer's own statement — names Perl 6 or Raku as the source. **Borrowed (circumstantial)** means timing and people make a link likely but nobody says so. **Considered, rejected** and **cited as prior art** cover records that name Perl 6 without taking from it. **Independent** means no link is documented and the adopter credits something else or nothing. **Common ancestor** means both sides descend from an earlier documented source, and **flowed into Perl 6** marks cases where Perl 6 was the importer. The absence of a Perl 6 mention was established by full-text search of the specific documents cited, not by an exhaustive grep of every proposal repository.

**Swift's multi-line strings are the one case in which a Perl 6 design decision shipped whole in a major language outside the Perl family.** On 25 April 2016, in the swift-evolution thread on multi-line string literals, Becca Royal-Gordon (then posting as Brent, and earlier a perl6-language participant and Parrot pumpking) described Perl 6's choice to take a heredoc's base indentation from its delimiter and asked whether Swift should do the same; on 5 May John Holdsworth replied that the lexer could strip the margin by the whitespace before the closing quote "as per Perl6" ([015738](https://lists.swift.org/pipermail/swift-evolution/Week-of-Mon-20160425/015738.html); [016646](https://lists.swift.org/pipermail/swift-evolution/Week-of-Mon-20160502/016646.html); [forum #21](https://forums.swift.org/t/optional-dictionary-subscripts/719/21)). Both became SE-0168's authors, with Tyler Cloutier. The proposal specifies that whitespace in front of the closing delimiter is removed from every line and never mentions Perl 6; in the April 2017 review Royal-Gordon summed up Perl 6's heredocs as Perl 5's plus de-indenting, and on 19 April Joe Groff accepted the proposal with the rule that the closing delimiter's position sets the indentation of the whole literal, making under-indentation an error where Raku only warns ([SE-0168](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0168-multi-line-string-literals.md); [review #17](https://forums.swift.org/t/review-se-0168-multi-line-string-literals/5611/17); [Accepted](https://forums.swift.org/t/accepted-se-0168-multi-line-string-literals/5715)). The credit lives where Swift regex's does, in the discussion rather than the proposal, but unlike the regex work the rule itself reached Swift 4.0 unchanged.

**Ruby's arrow is the best-documented case and sets the pattern for the rest.** The record is contemporaneous: Matz's diary listed a Perl6-style anonymous function among candidate lambda syntaxes on 1 July 2005 and on 22 July said the `->` proposal came from Perl 6, weighing Perl 6's cultural closeness to Ruby against doubt over when, or whether, Perl 6 would be used ([diary 1 Jul](http://www.rubyist.net/~matz/20050701.html); [diary 22 Jul](http://www.rubyist.net/~matz/20050722.html)). He committed it as new and very experimental on 27 July 2005 — five months after Pugs made pointy blocks runnable and three and a half years after A4 specified them — and two days later told ruby-dev that the grammar was aligned with Perl 6, in a comparison table that set Perl6/Ruby2 `-> (x,y) {...}` beside Groovy's and Haskell's forms ([ruby-dev:26650](https://blade.ruby-lang.org/ruby-dev/26650)). In May 2008, asked on ruby-core whether Perl 6 has the `->` syntax, he answered "Yes. It's the origin." Evan Phoenix had asked after quoting Matz's earlier remark that a syntax Ruby shared with Perl 6 would gain mind-share quickly; in reply Matz also noted that Perl 6 goes further, with `for obj -> var { }`, and that a `method(arg) -> x { }` block form he had tried himself was dropped ([ruby-core:16733](https://blade.ruby-lang.org/ruby-core/16733); [ruby-core:16734](https://blade.ruby-lang.org/ruby-core/16734); [ruby-core:16766](https://blade.ruby-lang.org/ruby-core/16766)). What crossed was only the token: Ruby's `->(x) { }` builds a lambda object, whereas Raku's `->` introduces a Block with a signature that `for`, `given` and `with` consume. **PHP's increment RFC** by George Peter Banyard is the only shipped mainstream feature whose design text says it follows Raku, and its framing is a survey: it treats Raku as a Perl successor whose string increment gives consistent results where PHP's does not ([PHP RFC](https://wiki.php.net/rfc/saner-inc-dec-operators)). A 2013 internals thread on letter decrementing had already cited S03's decrement, which does not fully mirror increment, and changed nothing ([68184](https://externals.io/message/68160#68184)).

**Swift's regex work is the deepest engagement and the clearest case of attrition between design and ship.** The interest started in the core team: Chris Lattner urged forum readers to study Perl 6's regexes from December 2015, wrote in January 2017 that Swift could learn a lot from Perl 6's grammars, and was still praising them in March 2020, while Joe Groff and Dave Abrahams pointed to Perl 6's approach and its recursive grammars in the same January 2017 thread ([335/4](https://forums.swift.org/t/empower-string-type-with-regular-expression/335/4); [876/9](https://forums.swift.org/t/empower-string-type-with-regular-expression/876/9); [4939/130](https://forums.swift.org/t/strings-in-swift-4/4939/130); [34373/10](https://forums.swift.org/t/regular-expressions-in-swift/34373/10); [4939/68](https://forums.swift.org/t/strings-in-swift-4/4939/68); [4939/153](https://forums.swift.org/t/strings-in-swift-4/4939/153)). Michael Ilseman's January 2018 "State of String" gist set Swift the goal of beating Perl at string processing, cited Apocalypse 5 as Perl's clean-up of its own regex mess, noted that Perl 6 has a PEG-like grammar system, and listed Perl 6 features Swift should take outright: whitespace-insignificant syntax, named captures, multi-line matching and regex interpolation. It also proposed ratcheting, frugal quantifiers and Raku's `%` separator quantifier, and asked whether alternation should use longest-token matching ([gist](https://gist.github.com/milseman/bb39ef7f170641ae52c13600a512782f)). By October 2021 Ilseman was explaining the retreat: familiarity with traditional syntax was decisive for a literal, so the team would spend its appetite for novelty on a separate `Pattern` type, even though he personally preferred Raku's syntax ([forum #46](https://forums.swift.org/t/declarative-string-processing-overview/52459/46)). In Hamish Knight's March 2022 regex-syntax pitch, Joe Groff's first reply again cited Raku for making `/x` the default ([Swift Forums](https://forums.swift.org/t/pitch-regex-syntax/55711/2)), and SE-0354 split the difference, making extended syntax mandatory in multi-line `#/…/#` literals while rejecting non-semantic whitespace for single-line ones ([SE-0354](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0354-regex-literals.md)). A5's 2002 choices thus reached Swift 5.7 two decades later in altered form — composable, named sub-patterns in RegexBuilder ([SE-0351](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0351-regex-builder.md)), PEG-style ordered choice credited to PEGs ([SE-0350](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0350-regex-type-overview.md)), and mandatory extended syntax in multi-line literals — while the attribution survives only in a gist and forum posts. SE-0363 even defines a `[raku]` reference link that its body never uses ([SE-0363](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0363-unicode-for-string-processing.md)). **Joose** is a genuine Perl 6 → other-language transmission, but it passed entirely through Moose and remained niche ([Joose manual](https://web.archive.org/web/20240208130151/http://www.openjsan.org/doc/s/sa/samuraijack/Joose/3013/lib/Joose/Manual.html)).

Two further transmissions sit below the language level, and neither counts as a language borrowing. Matthew Barnett, author of the third-party Python `regex` module, wrote in 2008 that his variable-length lookbehind, done by matching backwards, came from a page on how Perl 6 could implement it; he names no page, but S05 had described `after` as reversing the syntax tree since at least April 2006, and CPython never took the module into `re` ([cpython#48075](https://github.com/python/cpython/issues/48075#issuecomment-1093436869); [S05](https://github.com/Raku/old-design-docs/blob/master/S05-regex.pod); [PyPI regex](https://pypi.org/project/regex/)). And the Rust Grammar Working Group's notation took Raku's `%` and `%%` separator modifiers, which eddyb, then on Rust's language and compiler teams, attributed to Perl 6 in 2018; the wg-grammar files use `%%` throughout, while the Rust Reference keeps its own notation ([internals #8](https://internals.rust-lang.org/t/proposal-grammar-working-group/8442/8); [expr.lyg](https://github.com/rust-lang/wg-grammar/blob/master/grammar/expr.lyg)).

The pattern across these is consistent: when another community cites Raku approvingly, it is for **a small, cleanly reasoned semantic detail** — string increment, start-exclusive ranges, default rationals, Rat literals, grapheme-level dot, `/x` by default, `assuming`, `Range` matching, chained comparison, dual-precedence `and`/`&&`, Whatever placeholders, uncoloured functions — that appears as a row in a survey table rather than as a wholesale design. Core-team members argued from Perl 6 more often than the proposal archives show, but their own proposals died fast: Patrick Walton's `->` closures for Rust (2012) and Brad Fitzpatrick's `...` statement for Go (2019) were withdrawn within days, on grounds of consistency and tooling rather than provenance. The reasoned rejections are the most useful to an implementer, because each names a real cost: Royal-Gordon's that unrestricted junctions hide loops and their time complexity, Julia's that NFG pays off only for strings that start as UTF-32, and Don Syme's that junction-style comparison can be programmed without language support. PEP 3133 is the strongest evidence that Python's designers read Perl 6 during the Python 3000 period, and Guido van Rossum used it mainly to push PEP 3119 toward a more minimal design ([PEP 3133](https://peps.python.org/pep-3133/)). PEP 3134 is as close as Python comes to Perl 6 lineage in a shipped feature: its wording places RFC 88 beside Java, Ruby and C# as prior art rather than as the source ([PEP 344](https://peps.python.org/pep-0344/)), but its author's own python-dev message makes Perl 6's `@@` the only precedent he found, which stops just short of a borrowing ([python-dev](https://mail.python.org/archives/list/python-dev@python.org/message/GXDQLU2RAKBO35P2XOLRBR3YP6CPSGRB/)).

**Perl 5.10.0 (18 December 2007) is the largest documented import of Perl 6 design into another language's core**, yet its own perldelta credits none of it: the delta mentions Perl 6 only for the MAD converter and the `SVt_BIND` placeholder for implementing Perl 6 on Perl 5 ([perl5100delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5100delta.pod)), and the Perl Foundation's announcement spoke only generically of features and inspiration taken from the Perl 6 project ([Linux.com](https://www.linux.com/?p=512004)). The specific credits live in feature.pm, perlsyn and perlre ([perlre @5.10.0](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/perl-5.10.0/pod/perlre.pod)), and above all in the perl5-porters archive, whose threads name Perl 6 far more openly than the commits do. `//` came first: on 21 April 2002 Brent Dax — a Parrot developer who in 2001 had published Perl6::Parameters to emulate Perl 6 named parameters in Perl 5 — posted a patch titled as implementing Perl 6's `//` operator, six and a half months after A3 specified it ([msg57437](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2002/04/msg57437.html); [Perl6::Parameters](https://metacpan.org/pod/Perl6::Parameters)). The wish itself is older than Perl 6: Wilson Snyder pleaded for an `ordef`/`|||` defined-or in October 1999, with Larry Wall in the thread, and John Tobey posted a working `|||` patch in February 2000, so Perl 6 supplied the spelling rather than the idea ([msg1794](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/1999/10/msg1794.html); [msg7802](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2000/02/msg7802.html)). Dax's July repost asked whether other unobtrusive Perl 6 features should be backported, naming chained comparisons; Hugo van der Sanden, the 5.9 pumpking, replied that Larry had suggested routing such features through a `use perl6ish` pragma, Rafael Garcia-Suarez posted one in August, and the idea became `use feature` and its bundles in December 2005 — so the mechanism behind every later Perl 5 experiment was designed to import Perl 6 ([msg63819](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2002/07/msg63819.html); [msg64178](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2002/07/msg64178.html); [msg65716](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2002/08/msg65716.html); [msg107704](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2005/12/msg107704.html)). The low-precedence `err` from the same patch went the other way: kept because Perl 6 had it, it was dropped before 5.10.0 once Larry had renamed it `orelse` in Perl 6 in September 2007 ([msg107743](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2005/12/msg107743.html); [msg128324](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2007/09/msg128324.html)).

The 5.9.x work was an open port. Nigel Sandever and Steve Peters posted Perl 6-style `say` patches in 2005, and Rafael, the 5.10 pumpking, said he missed `say` partly because he played with Pugs; Damian Conway asked that any core switch follow Synopsis 4, and Robin Houston's given/when and `~~` followed the Perl 6 spec as closely as he found reasonable, with a smartmatch table adapted from S04; Rafael said in April 2006 that `state` came from Perl 6; and demerphq's April 2007 patch adding `\h` and redefining `\v` said both were from Perl 6 ([msg98796](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2005/03/msg98796.html); [msg106378](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2005/11/msg106378.html); [msg106978](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2005/11/msg106978.html); [msg107265](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2005/12/msg107265.html); [msg107579](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2005/12/msg107579.html); [msg111439](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2006/04/msg111439.html); [msg123648](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2007/04/msg123648.html)). Lexical `my $_` was a further 5.10.0 import that Nicholas Clark later said had been taken blindly from Perl 6, without the rest of the design it needs; it went experimental in 5.18 and was removed in 5.24.0 ([msg191632](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2012/09/msg191632.html); [perl5240delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5240delta.pod)). The perldelta's silence reflects writing convention, not an absence of borrowing. Chained comparisons are the exception that keeps the circumstantial verdict: Dax's 2002 backport suggestion is on record, but Zefram's 2017 ticket and the 67-message 2020 thread that produced 5.32's chaining name no other language at all ([msg248224](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2017/12/msg248224.html); [msg257005](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2020/02/msg257005.html)).

**The smartmatch saga is the cautionary tale for any implementer, because Perl 5 copied a specification that was still moving.** Today's perlsyn says the smartmatch table differs partly because the Raku spec changed after Perl 5 adopted it early, and it recommends Raku-style `any` instead ([perlsyn](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perlsyn.pod)). The risk was named at the time: Michael Schwern was uneasy in March 2005 about enshrining a Perl 6 feature that might change underneath Perl 5, and Houston's own patch adding perlsyn's section on differences from Perl 6 warned that the S04 table could not yet be treated as definitive ([msg98850](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2005/03/msg98850.html); [msg108013](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2005/12/msg108013.html)). On 3 January 2008, two weeks after 5.10.0, Larry Wall told p5p that Perl 6 had decided a year earlier that `~~` must not be symmetric and that arrays no longer imply `any`, and apologised that these "old misfeatures of the early Perl 6 design" had reached Perl 5; he had assumed p5p would track the design's principles rather than its features ([msg132467](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2008/01/msg132467.html)). Smartmatch was accordingly revised in 5.10.1 (22 August 2009) to make `~~` non-commutative, dispatching mainly on the right operand ([perl5101delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5101delta.pod)), and was made experimental in 5.18 (18 May 2013) ([perl5180delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5180delta.pod)); a December 2017 redesign was reverted because it broke too much of CPAN ([7896dde](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/7896dde7482a2851e73f0ac2c32d1c71f6e97dca)); `switch` was dropped from the 5.36 version bundle in 2022; 5.38 (2 July 2023) declared the experiment a failure and scheduled removal for 5.42 ([perl5380delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5380delta.pod)). The removal, written in July 2024, was committed to blead that August and reverted in February 2025 ([cb2167d](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/cb2167d3785e61e23819ca2c58ac1e89d5e4bf3b); [a215a77](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/a215a77dd95c2472a6395310fd80580c750260c2)), and 5.42 (3 July 2025) kept both behind `switch` and `smartmatch` feature flags with removal indefinitely postponed ([perl5420delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5420delta.pod)). perlexperiment now files them as features abandoned in place ([perlexperiment](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perlexperiment.pod)). The credit line faded in parallel: by 5.20 perlsyn described the switch as loosely based on an old version of the Perl 6 proposal and no longer resembling it, a 2020 sweep replaced the name Perl 6 with Raku, and from 5.34 feature.pm describes `say` as Raku-inspired ([perlsyn @v5.20.0](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/v5.20.0/pod/perlsyn.pod); [commit 17b3504](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/17b35041bdff13ad8301eae5df5f75578f910ce1); [feature.pm](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/lib/feature.pm)). One later delta explicitly accommodated Perl 6: 5.24.0 extended hashbang redirection to `perl6`, and 5.24.1 reverted it because it broke interpreter paths such as `/opt/perl64/bin/perl` ([perl5240delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5240delta.pod); [perl5241delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5241delta.pod)).

**CPAN carried more Perl 6 into Perl 5 than core did.** From 2001 to 2009 Damian Conway, Brent Dax, Luke Palmer, chromatic, Moritz Lenz and others published dozens of Perl6::* modules — Placeholders, Currying, Form, Export, Slurp, Gather, Say and Rules among Conway's alone, plus Perl6::Junction (2005), built from the Exegesis 6 design, and Perl6::Str (2008), a grapheme-level string API — and chromatic's Class::Roles, first released 12 December 2003, called itself a Perl 5 implementation of Perl 6 roles four months before A12 was published ([Class::Roles](https://metacpan.org/pod/Class::Roles); [Perl6::Junction](https://metacpan.org/pod/Perl6::Junction)). **Moose (2006) is the most consequential transfer**: its manual says it is based in large part on the Perl 6 object system plus ideas from CLOS and Smalltalk ([Moose::Manual](https://metacpan.org/pod/Moose::Manual)), and Class::MOP 0.01 lists the Pugs Perl 6 meta-model work as its prior art ([Class-MOP-0.01](https://fastapi.metacpan.org/source/STEVAN/Class-MOP-0.01/lib/Class/MOP.pm)). The keyword is circumstantial but strong: Moose 0.01's `has 'x' => (is => 'rw')` transliterates A12's `has $.y is rw`, whose declarator Wall chose to signal a has-a rather than an is-a relationship, and Stevan Little never said why he picked it ([Moose-0.01](https://fastapi.metacpan.org/source/STEVAN/Moose-0.01/lib/Moose.pm); [A12](https://www.perl.com/pub/2004/04/16/a12.html/)). Moose core developer Chris Prather said in 2012 that Little had thrown away the Pugs meta-models, that Moose's meta-model is very different from Perl 6's, and that only minor syntax such as `has` came from Perl 6 ([msg191706](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2012/09/msg191706.html)). Signatures took a three-hop route, from S06 to Method::Signatures (26 December 2007), MooseX::Method::Signatures (24 September 2008) and Kavorka (3 October 2013), each of which credits Perl 6, then to PPC0024 and 5.44's `:$name` parameters (15 July 2026) — though the PPC itself never names Raku ([MooseX::Method::Signatures](https://metacpan.org/pod/MooseX::Method::Signatures); [perl5440delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5440delta.pod)). The 5.38 `class` feature followed Paul Evans's Object::Pad test bed (2019), which began with the `has` keyword of Raku and Moose before switching to Corinna's `field` in 2022 ([Object::Pad Changes](https://metacpan.org/dist/Object-Pad/changes)); roles, the feature that started the whole Moose lineage, are still only a TODO in perlclass ([perlclass](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perlclass.pod)).

The 2012 signature debates turned that experience into doctrine. Nicholas Clark opened a thread titled "Perl 6 is not a menu", arguing that parts of a coherent design that had diverged from Perl 5 cannot be lifted back verbatim; demerphq judged nearly every Perl 6-inspired Perl 5 feature a failure, Leon Timmermans offered `say` and `//` as counter-examples, and Ricardo Signes, then pumpking, said porting Larry's designs blindly would be a disaster, while Reini Urban kept urging S06 as the model ([msg191632](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2012/09/msg191632.html); [msg191641](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2012/09/msg191641.html); [msg191642](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2012/09/msg191642.html); [msg191662](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2012/09/msg191662.html); [msg194385](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2012/10/msg194385.html)). Perl 6 served those debates mostly as a model not to copy, and Urban was still complaining in 2015 that Perl 5 had not followed Perl 6 for signatures ([msg230146](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2015/08/msg230146.html)).

Modern p5p routes features through CPAN prototypes and PPCs, and when Raku comes up it is usually as a reason to diverge: `defer` rejected the `LEAVE` name ([PPC0004](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0004-defer-block.md)), `any`/`all` rejected junction values ([PPC0027](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0027-any-and-all.md)), and PPC0005's everything-slices `@a[*]`, explicitly modelled on Raku's `*`, was rejected outright ([PPC0005](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0005-everything-slices.md)). In September 2026, asked whether the new match/case proposals avoided Raku's keywords on purpose, p5p answered that given/when are now permanently discouraged, so neither those names nor `~~` can be reused, and that any replacement must first solve smartmatch's number-versus-string ambiguity ([msg271262](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2026/09/msg271262.html); [msg271265](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2026/09/msg271265.html); [msg271274](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2026/09/msg271274.html)). Uncredited look-alikes such as `__SUB__` (5.16), lexical subs (5.18), the infix `isa` proposed in 2019 ([#17200](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/issues/17200)), `for my ($k, $v)` (5.36) and infix `^^` (5.40) have no Raku link in Perl's record, so the honest label for them is resemblance, not borrowing ([perl5400delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5400delta.pod)).

**Indentation-stripping heredocs are the clearest case of Perl 6 choosing the industry's eventual answer first, and the one case where the choice demonstrably travelled.** Apocalypse 2 accepted RFC 162's option of removing whitespace equal to the terminator's indentation in May 2001 ([A2](https://www.perl.com/pub/2001/05/03/wall.html/)). Swift 4 took that rule from Perl 6 through the SE-0168 authors, as the borrowings section shows; PHP 7.3, Java 15 and C# 11 settled on the same closing-delimiter rule more than fifteen years after A2 without citing Perl 6, even though a 2016 Roslyn comment had put a `q:to`-style heredoc to the C# team ([roslyn #2239](https://github.com/dotnet/roslyn/issues/2239#issuecomment-246411974)). Java's text-block JEP cites requests for Swift's literals, so a Perl 6 → Swift → Java path is possible, but nothing documents it ([JEP 378](https://openjdk.org/jeps/378)). Ruby 2.3 used a different, least-indented-line rule taken from Rails ([#9098](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/9098)). Perl 5.26's `<<~` is the telling hybrid, and it is documented after all: its commit credits nobody, but Matthew Horsfall opened the June 2016 p5p thread by proposing to follow Perl 6's stripping rules and said he had modelled the spelling lightly on Ruby 2.3; p5p then made mismatched indentation fatal where Raku warns and continues, so the rule that shipped is stricter than either source ([commit cdd6375](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/cdd6375d6dbc0eea3676faa4e615af1ae28bb103); [msg237477](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2016/06/msg237477.html); [msg237510](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/2016/07/msg237510.html); [perldiag](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perldiag.pod)). Ranges follow a different shape. Swift's internal release notes record its half-open operator moving from Ruby's `...` in February 2014 through a swap in May to `..<` on 23 June 2014, which they call precedented in Groovy; Groovy had adopted `..<` in April 2005 from Shijun He's proposal of 10 January 2005, which mentions only Ruby, and Kotlin's YouTrack request named Swift as its model ([Swift notes](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift/blob/swift-2.2-SNAPSHOT-2015-12-01-a/utils/buildbot-release-notes.txt); [groovy-dev](https://marc.info/?l=groovy-dev&m=113684773506831); [GroovyJSR notes](https://web.archive.org/web/20051220143509/http://docs.codehaus.org/display/GroovyJSR/2005/04/01/grammar+notes); [KT-15613](https://youtrack.jetbrains.com/issue/KT-15613)). Raku's `..^` (A12, April 2004) is not in that chain. C#, Ruby, Dart and Zig are the languages where Raku's start- and end-exclusive range operators were put in front of the designers and not taken up, and in Rust Perl 6's `..^` served as an argument against using that spelling for the inclusive range ([csharplang #185](https://github.com/dotnet/csharplang/issues/185#issuecomment-400886623); [#12133](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/12133); [dart-lang/language #2105](https://github.com/dart-lang/language/issues/2105#issuecomment-1105853005); [zig#359](https://github.com/ziglang/zig/issues/359#issuecomment-618326211); [rust#28237](https://github.com/rust-lang/rust/issues/28237#issuecomment-287577990)), and Groovy's `<..<`, the closest functional match to `^..^`, appears in the Kotlin KEEP only as Groovy prior art ([KEEP](https://github.com/Kotlin/KEEP/blob/master/proposals/open-ended-ranges.md)).

**Implicit parameters split into three families elsewhere, and none cites Raku.** By semantics — this pairing is our analysis, not a claim any source makes — Raku's positional placeholders (`$^a`) correspond to the numbered-hole family of Swift's `$0`, Ruby's `_1`, Elixir's `&1` and Clojure's `%1`; Groovy's, Kotlin's and Ruby 3.4's `it`, a single named parameter that may be used repeatedly (`{ it * it }`), corresponds to Raku's `$_` in a bare block, whose ancestor is Perl 5's `$_` in `map` and `grep`; and Scala's `_`, where each occurrence is a fresh parameter (`_ * _` takes two), is the analogue of WhateverCode. The documented origins point elsewhere. James Strachan added `it` to Groovy on 29 October 2003 with no stated source, and Andrey Breslav's talk on Kotlin's ancestry credits `it` to Groovy ([c525bfb](https://github.com/apache/groovy/commit/c525bfb600998c4e07805b4647a358153ff94174); [Breslav](https://downloads.ctfassets.net/oxjq45e8ilak/24vVb20Rpam2MksEU4QCOe/511bcd2575ba6e8af5cf82896db71570/AndreyBreslav_ShouldersOfGiants.pdf)). Swift's `$0` began as `_0`/`_1` in Lattner's July 2010 commit and was renamed on 18 September 2010 because Bertrand, almost certainly Bertrand Serlet, preferred `$0` ([6670786](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift/commit/667078650a35edaaec55c75b8b6a2bdfceb2eedb); [1bef5bd](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift/commit/1bef5bd648352c48baedc77f769c03a3a402fca0); [Lattner 2019](https://oleb.net/2019/chris-lattner-swift-origins/)). Scala's `_ + 1` arrived in version 2.5 in May 2007 ([Scala changelog](https://scala-lang.org/files/archive/spec/2.12/15-changelog.html)), more than five years after `$^a` and eight months after Whatever entered the spec, but neither the changelog nor the commits up to the v2.5.0 tag give a rationale ([scala v2.5.0](https://github.com/scala/scala/tree/v2.5.0)); Ruby's tickets mention Perl only as a readability warning ([#18980](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/18980); [#15723](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/15723)). Named-argument punning is the closest miss on the syntax side: Rust RFC 1682 considered a prefix-colon `S { :x }` form, nearly Raku's `:$x`, and attributed it to MoonScript ([RFC 1682](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rust-lang/rfcs/master/text/1682-field-init-shorthand.md)), while PEP 736's prior-art survey covers Ruby, ReasonML, SystemVerilog, Jakt, OCaml, JavaScript and Rust but not Raku ([PEP 736](https://peps.python.org/pep-0736/)).

**Signatures and CLIs show how timing alone misleads.** PEP 3102 added keyword-only arguments in April 2006, three years after A6 and a year before PEP 3133 proved that Python 3000 designers were reading Perl 6; yet it names no other language ([PEP 3102](https://peps.python.org/pep-3102/)), and the python-3000 threads that produced it settle the question. Their only Perl 6 remark is Guido's dismissal of a bracketed parameter syntax as emulating Perl 6, a day before he pronounced the bare `*`; nobody mentioned A6's named-only parameters, and the later PEP 3102 threads name no other language at all, so the verdict is independent invention ([001094](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-3000/2006-April/001094.html); [001150](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-3000/2006-April/001150.html); [python-dev](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-dev/2006-April/064598.html)). Rakudo shipped `MAIN` and `USAGE` in its 2010.06 release ([Rakudo ChangeLog](https://github.com/rakudo/rakudo/blob/main/docs/ChangeLog)); Michele Simionato's plac, which also generates a command line from a function signature, reached PyPI on 2 June 2010 — the same month ([plac](https://pypi.org/project/plac/)), and every later tool in that line, through argh, clize, Fire, Typer and Nim's cligen, descends from the Python side ([cligen](https://github.com/c-blake/cligen)). On paper Perl 6 was four years ahead: Larry Wall specified `MAIN`, multi-dispatch over `MAIN` candidates, signature-generated usage text and a `USAGE` override in one S06 commit on 10 August 2006, crediting only Unix switch conventions and Perl 5's `unless caller` idiom, and Rakudo ran a basic `MAIN` from 11 November 2008 ([msg25795](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/2006/08/msg25795.html); [41d13f8](https://github.com/rakudo/rakudo/commit/41d13f8eac2870031741179106cb35df668936dd)). Both requests to copy it that turned up were refused: Ruby's developer meeting rejected a Perl 6-like `main` in July 2012, and Go's Brad Fitzpatrick declined one in 2016 ([#5011](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/5011#note-7); [go#17178](https://github.com/golang/go/issues/17178)).

Gradual typing is where Perl 6 came closest to acknowledgement and still received none. Jeremy Siek's explainer names Perl 6 alongside Python 3k and ES4 as languages gradual typing should give a foundation to ([Siek](https://jsiek.github.io/home/WhatIsGradualTyping.html)), but the 2006 Siek & Taha paper itself mentions Common Lisp, Dylan, Cecil, Bigloo, Strongtalk and Thatte, not Perl ([Siek & Taha](http://schemeworkshop.org/2006/13-siek.pdf)). Perl 6's model — annotations add checked constraints and an unannotated variable means `Any` — is closer to Common Lisp-style optional typing than to Siek's sound-at-the-boundary gradual typing, so Wikipedia's uncited claim that Raku had gradual typing from the start is true only in the loose sense ([Wikipedia: Gradual typing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradual_typing)). The P0515 survey for C++20's `<=>` is a small but telling data point: it asserts that among major languages only Haskell returns a type from three-way comparison, overlooking Raku's `Order` enum ([P0515R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2017/p0515r0.pdf)), which fits the broader finding that designers' surveys rarely look at Raku at all.

**Roles are the best-documented common-ancestor case, and the direction is unambiguous.** A12 accepts the Traits paper's argument that classes should not manage both objects and code reuse and lists that paper as its only reference, while departing from it in three ways: roles may hold state (a point Wall defended on perl6-language in December 2003), conflicts are not resolved by renaming or excluding methods, and roles can be applied at runtime as mixins ([A12](https://www.perl.com/pub/2004/04/16/a12.html/); [perl6-language](https://groups.google.com/g/perl.perl6.language/c/kuxrQlzguIo)). The Traits authors then listed Perl 6 roles as an adopter in their 2006 TOPLAS paper, next to Scala ([TOPLAS 2006](https://scg.unibe.ch/archive/papers/Duca06bTOPLASTraits.pdf)). PHP, Java 8 and Groovy cite the Traits papers or the mixin tradition rather than Perl 6; Java's designers described default methods as a limited, stateless form of traits ([FOOL 2011](https://person.dibris.unige.it/zucca-elena/FOOL2011/goetz-abstract.html)). Multiple dispatch is the same story with CLOS in place of Squeak: Julia's 2012 paper places itself with CLOS, Dylan, Cecil and Diesel ([Julia 2012](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1209.5145)), and Stefan Karpinski later said that even CLOS was not a motivation and that Julia's resemblance to Fortress came from shared problems ([julia-dev](https://groups.google.com/g/julia-dev/c/NJ-b_KYZaIA)). Hyper-operators and Julia's dots both mark element-wise application syntactically instead of vectorizing implicitly — A3 made that choice in 2001 with a joke about APL, Julia made it against Chapel in 2017 citing Matlab and NumPy ([A3](https://www.perl.com/pub/2001/10/02/apocalypse3.html/); [Julia blog](https://julialang.org/blog/2017/01/moredots/)) — which is convergence on a principle, not transmission.

**KEEP/UNDO is the strongest unrewarded priority claim in control flow.** A4 specified blocks that run only on success or only on failure in January 2002 ([A4](https://www.perl.com/pub/2002/01/15/apo4.html/)); D shipped the same split as `on_scope_success` and `on_scope_failure` in DMD 0.148 on 25 February 2006 and respelled them `scope(success)` and `scope(failure)` in 0.149 on 7 March, with Walter Bright crediting Alexandrescu's C++ scope-guard macros and calling the feature one no other language had ([D changelog](https://digitalmars.com/d/1.0/changelog2.html); [D forum](https://forum.dlang.org/post/du106f$ptt$1@digitaldaemon.com)). That claim, four years after A4, is direct evidence that D's designer did not know KEEP/UNDO, and it fits his 2001 answer to whether D echoed Perl 6, that "any similarities are entirely coincidental" ([post](https://forum.dlang.org/post/9lsnrc$1kdo$1@digitaldaemon.com)), so D's look-alikes count as convergent on its designer's own word. Go's `defer` entered the spec in Robert Griesemer's commit of 27 January 2009, replacing a to-do item for an `onreturn/undo` statement, a lexical echo of UNDO with no evidence of a link, and Swift's `defer` (2015) is documented as Go's, tied to scope exit ([Go 4a903e0](https://github.com/golang/go/commit/4a903e0b32be5a590880ceb7379e68790602c29d); [Go 1593ab6](https://github.com/golang/go/commit/1593ab6bb4d5bf59bed1d66f292992572555b999); [ErrorHandlingRationale](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift/blob/main/docs/ErrorHandlingRationale.md)). Zig's `errdefer` (2018) grew out of Zig's own error sigils ([Zig](https://andrewkelley.me/post/zig-january-2018-in-review.html)). For an implementer the most useful distinction comes from Perl 5's PPC0004: Raku phasers are compile-time declarations activated by their presence, while `defer` is a statement that takes effect only if execution reaches it ([PPC0004](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0004-defer-block.md)). No counterpart to the ENTER, FIRST, NEXT or LAST phasers was found in any mainstream language.

Graphemes reverse the usual timing. Raku specified grapheme-level characters in 2002 but shipped them in April 2015, while Elixir made `String.length` grapheme-aware in November 2012 and Swift built `Character` on grapheme clusters in 2014, both grounded in UAX #29 with no Perl 6 citation ([Elixir changelog](https://github.com/elixir-lang/elixir/blob/v0.7.2/CHANGELOG.md); [Swift book](https://docs.swift.org/swift-book/documentation/the-swift-programming-language/stringsandcharacters/)); Ilseman later acknowledged Perl 6 only as a peer supporting grapheme-cluster regex semantics ([forum #100](https://forums.swift.org/t/declarative-string-processing-overview/52459/100)). **NFG's distinctive contribution — synthetic codepoints giving O(1) grapheme indexing — remains uncopied** ([MoarVM strings](https://github.com/MoarVM/MoarVM/blob/nqp-mbc/docs/strings.asciidoc)). Julia weighed it in writing during its 2016 string redesign and rejected it as sensible only for strings starting from UTF-32 and wasteful of memory; a Perl 6 developer in the same thread warned that the Parrot write-up being discussed had always been aspirational, so the rejection rested partly on that older text rather than on MoarVM ([#14383](https://github.com/JuliaLang/julia/pull/14383#issuecomment-172476026); [comment](https://github.com/JuliaLang/julia/pull/14383#issuecomment-172391604)). The `:D`/`:U` smileys likewise arrived after Spec# and Eiffel's attached types, and they are a binder-checked definedness constraint rather than a sum type or a flow analysis, so a claim that Perl 6 had null safety first is not defensible ([04840a3](https://github.com/Raku/old-design-docs/commit/04840a3a6967d9ee641a9a76a1f9ae929b0d0a19)). Grammars show influence flowing into Perl 6's vocabulary after the fact: Ford's 2004 PEG paper does not mention Perl ([Ford](https://bford.info/pub/lang/peg.pdf)), and Larry Wall added the description of Perl 6 patterns as a native PEG implementation to S05 only on 5 May 2010 ([52d2074](https://github.com/Raku/old-design-docs/commit/52d2074)).

**Many famous Perl 6 features were imported, often from Perl 5 CPAN.**

The switch module and its RFC appeared within three days of each other from the same author, so they were parallel prototypes rather than a clear sequence. The design documents also openly credit outside languages: Haskell for currying (RFC 23) and laziness (RFC 81) ([RFC 23](https://web.archive.org/web/2019/https://raku.org/archive/rfc/23.html)), APL for hyper-operators and Ada for subtypes, Prolog for the regex cut and for `:=` binding as a kind of unification ([A5](https://www.perl.com/pub/2002/06/04/apo5.html/)), Ruby for unary `*` flattening and, by Wall's own joke about turning up the Ruby-o-meter, for the July 2004 switch to `"{ expr }"` closure interpolation ([msg17885](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/2004/07/msg17885.html)), Java for interfaces (RFC 265), Unix command lines and Perl 5 I/O layers for colon-pair adverbs, Rx for Supplies ([Advent 2013](https://perl6advent.wordpress.com/2013/12/19/perl-6-supplies-reactive-programming/)), and — per Wall in 2016 — Haskell, Go, Erlang and C# for the concurrency model ([Slashdot 2016](https://developers.slashdot.org/story/16/07/14/1349207/the-slashdot-interview-with-larry-wall)). Three commonly repeated attributions — Icon for gather/take, Eiffel by name for PRE/POST, and Smalltalk — have no primary Perl 6 statement behind them and are third-party glosses.

**What nobody adopted is as informative as what spread.** Junctions have no adopter outside Perl 5 CPAN ports: Python's `any()`/`all()` are eager reductions, Perl 5.42 explicitly refused junction semantics ([PPC0027](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0027-any-and-all.md)), a Swift Language Workgroup member advised against them in 2023, F#'s designer declined junction-style comparison in 2022, and PHP and D saw only wishes and a library experiment ([Swift #10](https://forums.swift.org/t/pitch-simplify-substring-searching-with-custom-operators-and-predicates/63915/10); [F# #675](https://github.com/fsharp/fslang-suggestions/issues/675); [PHP](https://externals.io/message/92857#92870); [D](https://forum.dlang.org/post/igcoqh$1tir$1@digitalmars.com)). Hyper-operator syntax, Whatever-currying (raised for GHC only in an open 2026 pre-proposal, [#754](https://github.com/ghc-proposals/ghc-proposals/issues/754)), NFG synthetics, grammars as subclassable classes (only the `%` separator modifier reached a Rust grammar tool, [internals #8](https://internals.rust-lang.org/t/proposal-grammar-working-group/8442/8)), slangs, the ENTER/FIRST/NEXT/LAST phasers and the `:D`/`:U` smileys likewise have no documented counterpart elsewhere, and dynamically scoped `take` remains Raku's alone, since Kotlin's `sequence {}` forbids suspension from called functions by design and Python and JavaScript need `yield from`/`yield*` ([KEEP coroutines](https://github.com/Kotlin/KEEP/blob/master/proposals/coroutines.md)).

## Perl 6 traveled further as people, a VM and a warning

**Perl 6's best-documented influence on another language community ran through Pugs, and it affected Haskell's adoption rather than its design.** Haskell's HOPL III history (2007) names Darcs and Pugs as the open-source projects that drew people to Haskell, recounts that Tang picked Perl 6 as her exercise after reading *Types and Programming Languages*, and records Pugs's growth to about 200 contributors within a year ([HOPL III](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/history.pdf)). Tang adds that Pugs fed back on several GHC extensions and that people who met Haskell through Pugs, such as Edward Kmett, went on to energise its community ([Tang interview](https://andrewshitov.com/2015/05/05/interview-with-audrey-tang/)). Parrot left a cited footprint in VM research rather than in languages: the Lua 5.0 paper (2005) points to Parrot's planned register design as evidence of growing interest in register VMs while claiming Lua 5.0 as the first widely used one ([Lua 5.0](https://www.lua.org/doc/jucs05.pdf)), and the 2008 "Virtual Machine Showdown" paper says the stack-versus-register debate reopened because of Parrot's decision ([VM Showdown](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/10185/1/KC-Virtual-2008.pdf)). No hosted language other than Rakudo ever made Parrot its primary implementation, and active development ended in 2021 ([Wikipedia: Parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parrot_virtual_machine)).

**In other languages' design archives, Perl 6 is mostly a warning, and the warnings come from the top.** Guido van Rossum told python-dev in January 2005 that Python 3000 should not be as pervasive as the Perl 6 design, wrote in April 2006 that the Python 3000 process aimed to avoid a repeat of Perl 6, and in 2008 contrasted Python 3 with Perl 6 as a wholly new language and implementation ([python-dev](https://mail.python.org/archives/list/python-dev@python.org/message/HA6U5GSTAKNUH2BNZTEDEUCN7ARPDUAX/); [Artima](https://www.artima.com/weblogs/viewpost.jsp?thread=155123); [Linux Journal](https://www.linuxjournal.com/magazine/interview-guido-van-rossum)). In the python-3000 thread that led to keyword-only arguments he cut off a bracketed parameter syntax by reminding the list that "we don't want to emulate Perl 6!", and in 2007 he answered an argument for Unicode for its own sake by pointing to Perl 6 as where that road led ([001094](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-3000/2006-April/001094.html); [007689](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-3000/2007-May/007689.html)). Barry Warsaw in 2000 and Łukasz Langa in 2014 used Perl 6 the same way, and a 2007 April Fools' PEP joked about Perl 6 not yet existing ([Warsaw](https://mail.python.org/archives/list/python-dev@python.org/message/RP3CRS7I2GETZQ33XBD7FQ2H5PMNGGQP/); [Langa](https://mail.python.org/archives/list/python-dev@python.org/message/PGJMNZ5GVLAMOW3NFTRTP6OALAQFEHFY/); [PEP 3117](https://peps.python.org/pep-3117/)). Brendan Eich said in 2008 that nobody on ES4 wanted it to resemble Perl 6, and in 2014 granted that Perl 6 was full of good ideas that required a compatibility break ([esdiscuss 2008](https://esdiscuss.org/topic/complexity-tax); [esdiscuss 2014](https://esdiscuss.org/topic/thread-about-es6-on-reddit)).

The same use runs through the other leaderships. Ian Lance Taylor's Go 2 transitions design doc (2018) dates Perl 6 from 2000 to a 2015 spec, calls it intentionally incompatible and endlessly evolving, and draws the lesson "Don't be Perl 6." beside meeting deadlines and not changing everything at once; Go then gated language changes by the `go` directive in `go.mod` from Go 1.12, and Russ Cox later ruled out a Go 2 that breaks Go 1 programs ([28221-go2-transitions.md](https://github.com/golang/proposal/blob/master/design/28221-go2-transitions.md); [Go 1.12](https://go.dev/doc/go1.12); [Go blog](https://go.dev/blog/compat)). Douglas Gregor of Swift's Language Workgroup set Perl 5 → 6 beside Python 2 → 3 as a transition that never happened (2023), Martin Odersky contrasted Perl 6's years in announcement state with Scala 3's coming feature freeze (2019), Steve Klabnik, then on Rust's core team, wanted no repeat of the Ruby 1.9, Python 3 or Perl 6 situations (2015), and Walter Bright asked in 2015 whether Perl 6 had not gone through a paroxysm of its own ([Gregor](https://forums.swift.org/t/design-priorities-for-the-swift-6-language-mode/62408/57); [Odersky](https://contributors.scala-lang.org/t/move-slowly-and-iterate/2888/23); [Klabnik](https://users.rust-lang.org/t/twir-quote-of-the-week/328/187); [Bright](https://forum.dlang.org/post/me239u$1uj0$1@digitalmars.com)). In PHP, Adam Harvey warned in 2014 that PHP 7 risked becoming the Perl 6 of its decade, and Stanislav Malyshev opposed the 2016 pipe operator as moving the syntax too far toward Perl 6, though `|>` eventually shipped in PHP 8.5; PHP's 2014 vote to skip version 6 cited Perl 6 among its reasons ([76755](https://externals.io/message/76753#76755); [92968](https://externals.io/message/92960#92968); [pipe-operator-v3](https://wiki.php.net/rfc/pipe-operator-v3); [PHP RFC: php6](https://wiki.php.net/rfc/php6)). Rust's RFC threads mention it with Python 3 as the kind of drama a Rust 2.0 should avoid and, in the 2017 editions debate, as the model for separating a language (Perl 6) from its implementation (Rakudo) ([rfcs #886](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs/pull/886#issuecomment-272011711); [rfcs #2052](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs/pull/2052#issuecomment-312810251)); and Perl's own proposal process lists the Perl 6 RFC era's habit of proposing features nobody knew how to implement as something to avoid, while Rust modelled its RFC process on Python's PEPs ([PPC motivation](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/docs/motivation.md); [Rust RFC 0002](https://rust-lang.github.io/rfcs/0002-rfc-process.html)).

The histories other languages wrote about themselves point elsewhere too: JavaScript's HOPL IV history credits Perl 5 for its regex and array functions and Python for generators, destructuring and comprehensions, and never mentions Perl 6 ([HOPL JS](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/brendan-eich/js-hopl.pdf)); Chris Lattner's list of the languages that influenced Swift 1.0 omits Perl entirely, although he later urged Perl 6's regexes and grammars on Swift's string work ([nondot.org](https://nondot.org/sabre/)). As of November 2026 Wikidata records no language as influenced by Raku ([Wikidata Q2052676](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q2052676)). Raku's Wikipedia infobox lists Perl and Haskell as influenced, citing Audrey Tang's 2010 post on implementing Perl 6 — not a talk but her PerlMonks reply of 21 April 2010, reposted on her blog — which supports influence on Perl 5 through Moose and other CPAN releases but makes no claim about Haskell: it says Pugs needed host-language features that later appeared in Haskell 2010 and GHC, and one of them, pattern guards, was already documented in GHC 4.08.1 in 2000 ([Wikipedia: Raku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raku_(programming_language)); [Tang](https://web.archive.org/web/20100425002903/http://pugs.blogs.com/pugs/2010/04/how-to-implement-perl-6-in-10.html); [GHC 4.08.1](https://downloads.haskell.org/~ghc/old_docs/old_4.08.1_head/set/pattern-guards.html)). Haskell's listing of Raku cites a Perl 6 wiki glossary page ([Wikipedia: Haskell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskell)). The Raku community itself has claimed almost nothing: across 1,088 Weekly and Advent posts from 2009 to 2026, 133 6guts posts, raku.org and the docs FAQ, no author says a language outside the Perl family borrowed from Perl 6, and the Weekly never reported PHP's increment RFC or C#'s chained-comparison proposal; the one outward claim the record confirms is small, Sidef, a single-author language whose readme names Raku among its inspirations ([rakuweekly.blog](https://rakuweekly.blog/); [raku-advent.blog](https://raku-advent.blog/); [Raku FAQ](https://docs.raku.org/language/faq); [Sidef](https://github.com/trizen/sidef)). Larry Wall himself presented Perl 6 as a borrower, saying in 2016 that other languages had stolen Perl 5's regexes and that any language borrowing enough of Perl 6 to be consistent would simply have reimplemented it; his 2006 prediction that everyone would move from PCRE to Perl 6 regexes was not borne out even by Swift, the one language that seriously tried ([Slashdot 2016](https://developers.slashdot.org/story/16/07/14/1349207/the-slashdot-interview-with-larry-wall); [OSDC::Israel 2006](https://perl.org.il/presentations/larry-wall-present-continuous-future-perfect/transcript.html)).

## Conclusion

The evidence separates what Perl 6 got right from what it transmitted. Its specification picked several answers the industry later converged on, five to twenty years early — the closing-delimiter heredoc rule and defined-or in 2001, success/failure scope blocks, `/x` by default and grammars as classes in 2002, named-only parameters in 2003 — but almost none of that judgment turned into citations, because ideas travel with shippable implementations and with the languages designers already use. The heredoc rule, Perl 6's 2001 choice, is the one design decision that shipped intact in a major non-Perl language, and even there the credit survives only in the swift-evolution thread, not in SE-0168. What did cross the boundary, in five documented borrowings and Perl 5's many, was **small, separable syntax or semantics that a host language could adopt without buying into the rest**: an arrow token, a heredoc margin rule, a string-increment rule, a regex whitespace default, a CPAN object system, and below the language level a lookbehind technique and a separator modifier. The features that demand whole-language commitment — junction autothreading, hyper and meta-operators, Whatever-currying, NFG, grammar subclassing, slangs — have not moved at all, and their non-adoption measures how much of Raku is architecture rather than syntax.

Two practical lessons follow for a Raku implementer. Priority claims need the right date: the Apocalypse establishes the idea, but Pugs (2005) and Rakudo (2009–2015) decide whether anyone outside could have used it, and late-specified features such as `:D`, NFG and Supplies were contemporaries of their analogues, not ancestors; even `sub MAIN`, specified four years before plac, became usable in full only the month plac appeared. And Perl 5's smartmatch is the case study in borrowing too early — it froze a 2002–2007 snapshot of a design Raku then revised, drew Larry Wall's own apology within weeks of shipping, and spent eighteen years unable to fix or remove it. The pieces of Raku now being cited in PHP, Ruby, Swift, C#, Carbon and TC39 discussions — range endpoints, rational defaults, chained comparison, regex ergonomics — are exactly the settled, separable ones, which suggests that stable and well-documented semantics for such pieces, rather than the ambition of the whole design, are what other languages will continue to pick up.

## How this was researched

**A full-text search of the proposal archives found no hidden borrowing; the discussion archives found one.** On 7 November 2026 the complete [swift-evolution](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution), [rust-lang/rfcs](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs), [Kotlin KEEP](https://github.com/Kotlin/KEEP), [dotnet/csharplang](https://github.com/dotnet/csharplang) (proposals and design-meeting notes), Scala SIP ([current](https://github.com/scala/improvement-proposals) and [historical](https://github.com/scala/docs.scala-lang)) and [dart-lang/language](https://github.com/dart-lang/language) repositories were searched for Perl 6, Perl6, Raku and Rakudo, along with their GitHub issue and pull-request threads and the JEPs. Only four files matched: the C# chained-comparison proposal, Rust RFC 2909, a Kotlin design note on function colouring, and SE-0363's unused link. The issue threads added the Dart range survey and a handful of Rust comments. All of these are prior art or suggestions not taken, so none changes a verdict, and every other Perl mention in those archives concerns Perl 5 regexes, quoting or tooling. JEP coverage is near-complete where it matters. openjdk.org began refusing requests from this machine after 106 of its 511 pages had arrived, and Wayback Machine snapshots supplied 122 more, so 228 JEPs were read, including 157 of the 165 language, javac and core-library ones; the rest are HotSpot, GC, security, networking and tooling JEPs ([JEP index](https://openjdk.org/jeps/0)). No JEP read mentions Perl 6 or Raku, and [JEP 111](https://openjdk.org/jeps/111)'s Perl references are to Perl 5.10-era regex constructs.

A second pass the same day covered the venues where design is argued rather than recorded. Seventeen Discourse forums — among them forums.swift.org with the 2015–2017 swift-evolution list imported into it, internals.rust-lang.org, contributors.scala-lang.org and discuss.python.org — were searched exhaustively for the same terms, along with csharplang Discussions, Ruby's Redmine tracker, PHP internals through externals.io, and the D and Nim forums ([forums.swift.org](https://forums.swift.org/search.json?q=raku); [bugs.ruby-lang.org](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/search?q=%22perl+6%22&issues=1); [externals.io](https://externals.io/search?q=%22perl+6%22); [forum.dlang.org](https://forum.dlang.org/search?q=perl6)). The mailing lists were downloaded and grepped locally: the subject lines of all 271,342 perl5-porters articles from August 1999 to November 2026 plus about 2,000 thread bodies chosen by subject, the full text of python-3000, python-ideas and python-dev, es-discuss in full from 2006 to 2016 and by its mirror's search to 2021, and ruby-dev around Matz's 2005 commit ([p5p](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl5.porters/); [python-3000](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-3000/); [es-discuss](https://www.mail-archive.com/es-discuss@mozilla.org/)). Twenty more design and documentation repositories — Carbon, the Go, GHC, OCaml, F#, PowerShell and Nim proposal archives, TC39's lists, spec and meeting notes, the Julia, Elixir, Zig, Gleam, Crystal, Mojo and TypeScript docs, and the PEPs — plus 329 TC39 proposal repositories and 17 issue trackers were grepped too; outside the PEPs only three files matched, the Go 2 transitions design doc, Carbon's p0680 and the TC39 Decimal README, and the trackers yielded about 55 genuine threads ([golang/proposal](https://github.com/golang/proposal); [carbon-lang](https://github.com/carbon-language/carbon-lang); [tc39/proposals](https://github.com/tc39/proposals)). The Raku side was checked against the perl6-language archive (36,873 articles), the Pugs svn mirror, and 1,088 Weekly and Advent posts ([perl6-language](https://www.nntp.perl.org/group/perl.perl6.language/)).

That pass produced one verdict-changing borrowing (Swift's heredoc rule), the perl5-porters credits for Perl 5's imports, two transmissions below the language level and most of the near-miss rows. As the first round had predicted, the p5p threads behind `//` and `<<~` held the missing credits, while those behind Perl 5's chained comparisons and the python-3000 threads behind PEP 3102 did not. It also closed the earlier gaps on undocumented look-alikes with non-Perl origins: Swift's public history from July 2010 dates `$0`, `\( )` and `??` and credits Groovy for `..<` and Go for `defer`; Breslav credits Groovy for Kotlin's `it` and Elvis; ES2015's strawmen trace shorthand properties to SpiderMonkey and template literals to E, PHP and shell-style interpolation; and Guido traced `any`/`all` to ABC ([Swift notes](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift/blob/swift-2.2-SNAPSHOT-2015-12-01-a/utils/buildbot-release-notes.txt); [ErrorHandlingRationale](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift/blob/main/docs/ErrorHandlingRationale.md); [Breslav](https://downloads.ctfassets.net/oxjq45e8ilak/24vVb20Rpam2MksEU4QCOe/511bcd2575ba6e8af5cf82896db71570/AndreyBreslav_ShouldersOfGiants.pdf); [harmony:quasis](https://web.archive.org/web/20111223164326/http://wiki.ecmascript.org/doku.php?id=harmony:quasis); [Artima](https://www.artima.com/weblogs/viewpost.jsp?thread=98196)). Only C#'s `??` still has no origin statement, though it is now dated to the May 2004 C# 2.0 specification ([Gunnerson](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/ericgu/updated-c-v2-0-specifications-now-available)). Still unsearched: Elixir Forum's own search (blocked), Reddit (HTTP 403), news.php.net (unreachable), IRC logs and conference talks, golang-nuts and golang-dev, discuss.python.org beyond a keyword search, Kotlin's YouTrack and Slack, TC39 Discourse, the Mojo Discord, Perl's PPC discussions on GitHub, p5p bodies under unrelated subjects, es-discuss after October 2016 beyond its mirror's search, ruby-talk and later ruby-dev, and Swift's WWDC 2014 sessions and podcasts.
