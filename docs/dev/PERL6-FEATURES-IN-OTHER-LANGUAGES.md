# Most Languages Reinvented What Perl 6 Designed

Perl 6 specified most of its signature features between Larry Wall's July 2000 announcement and Apocalypse 12 in April 2004, often years before C#, Swift, Kotlin, Rust, Julia or modern Python shipped look-alikes, yet outside the Perl family the adopting languages' own design records document **only four borrowings, and each was partial**: Ruby took the `->` lambda arrow (committed July 2005, shipped in 1.9.0 in December 2007) but not pointy-block semantics; PHP 8.3 aligned string increment with Raku's behaviour (RFC accepted 25–0 in July 2023); Apple's Swift string lead planned in 2018 to take whitespace-insensitive patterns, interpolation and ratcheting from Perl 6, then shipped PCRE-style literals in Swift 5.7 (2022); and JavaScript's Joose reached the Perl 6 object model only through Perl 5's Moose. **Perl 5 is the one real importer** — `say`, given/when, `~~`, two backtracking verbs, Moose, Regexp::Grammars and the design of the 5.38 `class` feature all trace to Perl 6 — and its smartmatch, copied from a specification that kept moving, was declared a failed experiment in 2023 and survives only behind feature flags. Nearly every other overlap examined, across roughly thirty languages, is either **independent reinvention** (indentation-stripping heredocs, half-open ranges, implicit lambda parameters, keyword-only arguments, CLIs generated from signatures, optional typing) or **descent from an ancestor Perl 6 shared** (the 2003 Traits paper, CLOS and Dylan, APL and NumPy, CLU and Icon generators, Unix pipes, Ada subtypes, Unicode UAX #29, C# async and Rx). Several famous Perl 6 features were themselves imports: junctions and switch began as Damian Conway's Perl 5 modules in August 2000, roles came from the Traits paper, and Supplies came from Rx. The cause is timing and citation habit: Perl 6 was first on paper but had no stable release until December 2015, so designers cited the languages that shipped — Python, C#, Haskell, Perl 5 — and in other communities' archives Perl 6 appears more often as a cautionary tale about breaking compatibility than as a source. A November 2026 full-text search of the Swift, Rust, Kotlin, C#, Scala and Dart proposal archives and the JEPs found Raku only as prior art — most visibly in C#'s chained-comparison proposal, approved in April 2026 — and never as the stated source of a shipped feature. **Raku's most distinctive ideas — junctions, hyper-operators, Whatever-currying, NFG synthetic graphemes, grammars as classes and slangs — have no documented adopter outside the Perl family.**

## Perl 6 led on paper by years but shipped a decade late

Any priority claim depends on which of three Perl 6 dates one uses. The proposal layer is the **361 RFCs** submitted between 1 August and late September 2000, after Larry Wall announced the redesign at the State of the Onion on 19 July 2000 ([RFC index](https://web.archive.org/web/20191117000414/https://raku.org/archive/rfc/); [Wikipedia: Raku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raku_(programming_language))). The specification layer is Wall's Apocalypses, Damian Conway's Exegeses and the Synopses; only A1–A6 (April 2001 – March 2003) and A12 (16 April 2004) were ever written, and the Synopses began with S05 in June 2002 and were maintained until about 2015 ([A12](https://www.perl.com/pub/2004/04/16/a12.html/); [S05](https://github.com/Raku/old-design-docs/blob/master/S05-regex.pod)). The implementation layer starts with Pugs, Audrey Tang's Haskell interpreter, first released on **7 February 2005**: it ran junctions and chained comparisons in its first release and had multis, signatures, rules via PGE, reduce, hyper-operators and gather/take by late May ([Pugs ChangeLog](https://github.com/audreyt/pugs/blob/master/ChangeLog)). Rakudo was named in January 2008, released independently of Parrot from February 2009, shipped Rakudo Star on **29 July 2010** and the first stable language version, **6.c, on 25 December 2015**, followed by 6.d in November 2018 ([Michaud](https://raku.github.io/CCR/Remaster/Patrick%20Michaud/The-compiler-formerly-known-as-raku.html); [TPF: Rakudo Star](https://news.perlfoundation.org/post/rakudo_star_-_a_useful_usable); [Rakudo 2015.12](https://github.com/rakudo/rakudo/blob/main/docs/announce/2015.12.md); [Rakudo 2018.11](https://github.com/rakudo/rakudo/blob/main/docs/announce/2018.11.md)). As of Rakudo 2026.09 (28 September 2026) RakuAST is the default frontend, but 6.e is still a preview ([Rakudo 2026.09](https://github.com/rakudo/rakudo/blob/main/docs/announce/2026.09.md)).

Two caveats matter for anyone weighing priority claims. First, Apocalypse decisions were often reversed: A3 (October 2001) defined binary `~~` as logical XOR and A4 (January 2002) spelled smartmatch `=~`; smartmatch became `~~` only by A6 in March 2003 ([A3](https://www.perl.com/pub/2001/10/02/apocalypse3.html/); [A4](https://www.perl.com/pub/2002/01/15/apo4.html/); [A6](https://www.perl.com/pub/2003/03/07/apocalypse6.html/)). The earliest Apocalypse mention is the fair date for when an idea was first specified, but the final semantics usually come from later Synopsis edits. Second, a block of features commonly treated as Perl 6 originals arrived late enough to be contemporaries of their look-alikes elsewhere, or later than them:

Spec dates before April 2006 come only from the Apocalypses and Exegeses, because the spec's git history begins with an April 2006 mirror and the canonical November 2008 import; "≤" below marks a date by which a feature was already present.

The verdicts use a fixed scale. **Borrowed (documented)** means the adopter's own record — a PEP, RFC, KEEP, Swift Evolution text, commit message, or the designer's own statement — names Perl 6 or Raku as the source. **Borrowed (circumstantial)** means timing and people make a link likely but nobody says so. **Considered, rejected** and **cited as prior art** cover records that name Perl 6 without taking from it. **Independent** means no link is documented and the adopter credits something else or nothing. **Common ancestor** means both sides descend from an earlier documented source, and **flowed into Perl 6** marks cases where Perl 6 was the importer. The absence of a Perl 6 mention was established by full-text search of the specific documents cited, not by an exhaustive grep of every proposal repository.

**Ruby's arrow is the cleanest case and sets the pattern for the rest.** Matz committed it as new and very experimental on 27 July 2005 — five months after Pugs made pointy blocks runnable and three and a half years after A4 specified them — and in May 2008, asked on ruby-core whether Perl 6 has the `->` syntax, he answered "Yes. It's the origin." He added that a syntax shared by Ruby and Perl 6 would gain mind-share quickly, and that he had tried Perl 6's more radical `method(arg) -> x { }` block form and abandoned it as too radical ([ruby-core:16733](https://blade.ruby-lang.org/ruby-core/16733); [ruby-core:16734](https://blade.ruby-lang.org/ruby-core/16734); [ruby-core:16766](https://blade.ruby-lang.org/ruby-core/16766)). What crossed was only the token: Ruby's `->(x) { }` builds a lambda object, whereas Raku's `->` introduces a Block with a signature that `for`, `given` and `with` consume. **PHP's increment RFC** by George Peter Banyard is the only shipped mainstream feature whose design text says it follows Raku, and its framing is a survey: it treats Raku as a Perl successor whose string increment gives consistent results where PHP's does not ([PHP RFC](https://wiki.php.net/rfc/saner-inc-dec-operators)).

**Swift's regex work is the deepest engagement and the clearest case of attrition between design and ship.** Michael Ilseman's January 2018 "State of String" gist set Swift the goal of beating Perl at string processing, cited Apocalypse 5 as Perl's clean-up of its own regex mess, noted that Perl 6 has a PEG-like grammar system, and listed Perl 6 features Swift should take outright: whitespace-insignificant syntax, named captures, multi-line matching and regex interpolation. It also proposed ratcheting, frugal quantifiers and Raku's `%` separator quantifier, and asked whether alternation should use longest-token matching ([gist](https://gist.github.com/milseman/bb39ef7f170641ae52c13600a512782f)). By October 2021 Ilseman was explaining the retreat: familiarity with traditional syntax was decisive for a literal, so the team would spend its appetite for novelty on a separate `Pattern` type, even though he personally preferred Raku's syntax ([forum #46](https://forums.swift.org/t/declarative-string-processing-overview/52459/46)). Joe Groff's March 2022 regex-syntax pitch again cited Raku for making `/x` the default ([Swift Forums](https://forums.swift.org/t/pitch-regex-syntax/55711)), and SE-0354 split the difference, making extended syntax mandatory in multi-line `#/…/#` literals while rejecting non-semantic whitespace for single-line ones ([SE-0354](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0354-regex-literals.md)). A5's 2002 choices thus reached Swift 5.7 two decades later in altered form — composable, named sub-patterns in RegexBuilder ([SE-0351](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0351-regex-builder.md)), PEG-style ordered choice credited to PEGs ([SE-0350](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0350-regex-type-overview.md)), and mandatory extended syntax in multi-line literals — while the attribution survives only in a gist and forum posts. SE-0363 even defines a `[raku]` reference link that its body never uses ([SE-0363](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0363-unicode-for-string-processing.md)). **Joose** is a genuine Perl 6 → other-language transmission, but it passed entirely through Moose and remained niche ([Joose manual](https://www.openjsan.org/doc/s/sa/samuraijack/Joose/3013/lib/Joose/Manual.html)).

The pattern across these is consistent: when another community cites Raku approvingly, it is for **a small, cleanly reasoned semantic detail** — string increment, start-exclusive ranges, default rationals, grapheme-level dot, `/x` by default, `assuming`, `Range` matching, chained comparison, uncoloured functions — that appears as a row in a survey table rather than as a wholesale design. PEP 3133 is the strongest evidence that Python's designers read Perl 6 during the Python 3000 period, and Guido van Rossum used it mainly to push PEP 3119 toward a more minimal design ([PEP 3133](https://peps.python.org/pep-3133/)). PEP 3134 is as close as Python comes to Perl 6 lineage in a shipped feature, and its wording places RFC 88 beside Java, Ruby and C# as prior art rather than as the source ([PEP 344](https://peps.python.org/pep-0344/)).

**Perl 5.10.0 (18 December 2007) is the largest documented import of Perl 6 design into another language's core**, yet its own perldelta credits none of it: the delta mentions Perl 6 only for the MAD converter and the `SVt_BIND` placeholder for implementing Perl 6 on Perl 5 ([perl5100delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5100delta.pod)), and the Perl Foundation's announcement spoke only generically of features and inspiration taken from the Perl 6 project ([Linux.com](https://www.linux.com/?p=512004)). The specific credits live in feature.pm, perlsyn and perlre ([perlre @5.10.0](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/perl-5.10.0/pod/perlre.pod)). For `//` the circumstantial case is strong: A3 specified it in October 2001, and nine months later Brent Dax — who in 2001 had published Perl6::Parameters to emulate Perl 6 named parameters in Perl 5 — submitted the defined-or patch ([commit c963b15](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/c963b15115); [Perl6::Parameters](https://metacpan.org/pod/Perl6::Parameters)). The same holds for `state`, which appeared in E7 and Pugs before 5.10.

**The smartmatch saga is the cautionary tale for any implementer, because Perl 5 copied a specification that was still moving.** Today's perlsyn says the smartmatch table differs partly because the Raku spec changed after Perl 5 adopted it early, and it recommends Raku-style `any` instead ([perlsyn](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perlsyn.pod)). Smartmatch was significantly revised in 5.10.1 (22 August 2009) and made experimental in 5.18 (18 May 2013) ([perl5180delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5180delta.pod)); a December 2017 redesign was reverted because it broke too much of CPAN ([7896dde](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/7896dde7482a2851e73f0ac2c32d1c71f6e97dca)); `switch` was dropped from the 5.36 version bundle in 2022; 5.38 (2 July 2023) declared the experiment a failure and scheduled removal for 5.42 ([perl5380delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5380delta.pod)). The removal was committed to blead in July 2024 and reverted in February 2025 ([cb2167d](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/cb2167d3785e61e23819ca2c58ac1e89d5e4bf3b); [a215a77](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/a215a77dd95c2472a6395310fd80580c750260c2)), and 5.42 (3 July 2025) kept both behind `switch` and `smartmatch` feature flags with removal indefinitely postponed ([perl5420delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5420delta.pod)). perlexperiment now files them as features abandoned in place ([perlexperiment](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perlexperiment.pod)). The credit line faded in parallel: by 5.20 perlsyn described the switch as loosely based on an old version of the Perl 6 proposal and no longer resembling it, a 2020 sweep replaced "Perl 6" with "Raku", and from 5.34 feature.pm describes `say` as Raku-inspired ([perlsyn @v5.20.0](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/v5.20.0/pod/perlsyn.pod); [commit 17b3504](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/17b35041bdff13ad8301eae5df5f75578f910ce1); [feature.pm](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/lib/feature.pm)). One later delta explicitly accommodated Perl 6: 5.24.0 extended hashbang redirection to `perl6`, and 5.24.1 reverted it because it broke interpreter paths such as `/opt/perl64/bin/perl` ([perl5240delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5240delta.pod); [perl5241delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5241delta.pod)).

**CPAN carried more Perl 6 into Perl 5 than core did.** From 2001 to 2009 Damian Conway, Brent Dax, Luke Palmer, chromatic, Moritz Lenz and others published dozens of Perl6::* modules — Placeholders, Currying, Form, Export, Slurp, Gather, Say and Rules among Conway's alone, plus Perl6::Junction (2005), built from the Exegesis 6 design, and Perl6::Str (2008), a grapheme-level string API — and chromatic's Class::Roles, first released 12 December 2003, called itself a Perl 5 implementation of Perl 6 roles four months before A12 was published ([Class::Roles](https://metacpan.org/pod/Class::Roles); [Perl6::Junction](https://metacpan.org/pod/Perl6::Junction)). **Moose (2006) is the most consequential transfer**: its manual says it is based in large part on the Perl 6 object system plus ideas from CLOS and Smalltalk ([Moose::Manual](https://metacpan.org/pod/Moose::Manual)). Signatures took a three-hop route, from S06 to Method::Signatures (26 December 2007), MooseX::Method::Signatures (24 September 2008) and Kavorka (3 October 2013), each of which credits Perl 6, then to PPC0024 and 5.44's `:$name` parameters (15 July 2026) — though the PPC itself never names Raku ([MooseX::Method::Signatures](https://metacpan.org/pod/MooseX::Method::Signatures); [perl5440delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5440delta.pod)). The 5.38 `class` feature followed Paul Evans's Object::Pad test bed (2019), which began with the `has` keyword of Raku and Moose before switching to Corinna's `field` in 2022 ([Object::Pad Changes](https://metacpan.org/dist/Object-Pad/changes)); roles, the feature that started the whole Moose lineage, are still only a TODO in perlclass ([perlclass](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perlclass.pod)).

Modern p5p routes features through CPAN prototypes and PPCs, and when Raku comes up it is usually as a reason to diverge: `defer` rejected the `LEAVE` name ([PPC0004](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0004-defer-block.md)), `any`/`all` rejected junction values ([PPC0027](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0027-any-and-all.md)), and PPC0005's everything-slices `@a[*]`, explicitly modelled on Raku's `*`, was rejected outright ([PPC0005](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0005-everything-slices.md)). Uncredited look-alikes such as `__SUB__` (5.16), lexical subs (5.18), `for my ($k, $v)` (5.36) and infix `^^` (5.40) have no Raku link in Perl's record, so the honest label for them is resemblance, not borrowing ([perl5400delta](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/blob/blead/pod/perl5400delta.pod)).

**Indentation-stripping heredocs are the clearest case of Perl 6 choosing the industry's eventual answer first.** Apocalypse 2 accepted RFC 162's option of removing whitespace equal to the terminator's indentation in May 2001 ([A2](https://www.perl.com/pub/2001/05/03/wall.html/)); Swift 4, PHP 7.3, Java 15 and C# 11 all settled on a closing-delimiter rule more than fifteen years later without citing Perl 6, while Ruby 2.3 used a different, least-indented-line rule taken from Rails ([#9098](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/9098)). Perl 5.26's `<<~` is the telling hybrid: exactly A2's terminator rule with Ruby's spelling, and a commit that credits neither ([commit cdd6375](https://github.com/Perl/perl5/commit/cdd6375d6dbc0eea3676faa4e615af1ae28bb103)). Ranges follow the same shape. Swift renamed its half-open operator to `..<` in July 2014 for clarity with no stated source ([AppDeveloperMagazine](https://appdevelopermagazine.com/apple-opens-new-swift-blog,-announces-xcode-6-beta-3-for-ios-development/)), and Kotlin's YouTrack request named Swift as its model ([KT-15613](https://youtrack.jetbrains.com/issue/KT-15613)). C#, Ruby and Dart are the three languages where Raku's start- and end-exclusive range operators were put in front of the designers and not taken up ([csharplang #185](https://github.com/dotnet/csharplang/issues/185#issuecomment-400886623); [#12133](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/12133); [dart-lang/language #2105](https://github.com/dart-lang/language/issues/2105#issuecomment-1105853005)), and Groovy's `<..<`, the closest functional match to `^..^`, appears in the Kotlin KEEP only as Groovy prior art ([KEEP](https://github.com/Kotlin/KEEP/blob/master/proposals/open-ended-ranges.md)).

**Implicit parameters split into two families elsewhere, and neither cites Raku.** Raku's positional placeholders (`$^a`) correspond to the numbered-hole family — Swift's `$0`, Ruby's `_1`, Elixir's `&1`, Clojure's `%1` — and Whatever-currying (`* + 1`) to the single-anonymous-hole family of Scala's `_` and Kotlin's and Ruby's `it`. Scala's `_ + 1` arrived in version 2.5 in May 2007 ([Scala changelog](https://scala-lang.org/files/archive/spec/2.12/15-changelog.html)), more than five years after `$^a` and in the same period Raku was designing Whatever (first in the spec in September 2006), but nothing links them; Ruby's tickets mention Perl only as a readability warning ([#18980](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/18980); [#15723](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/15723)). Named-argument punning is the closest miss on the syntax side: Rust RFC 1682 considered a prefix-colon `S { :x }` form, nearly Raku's `:$x`, and attributed it to MoonScript ([RFC 1682](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rust-lang/rfcs/master/text/1682-field-init-shorthand.md)), while PEP 736's prior-art survey covers Ruby, ReasonML, SystemVerilog, Jakt, OCaml, JavaScript and Rust but not Raku ([PEP 736](https://peps.python.org/pep-0736/)).

**Signatures and CLIs show how timing alone misleads.** PEP 3102 added keyword-only arguments in April 2006, three years after A6 and a year before PEP 3133 proved that Python 3000 designers were reading Perl 6; yet it names no other language, so independent invention is the better-supported verdict and circumstantial influence the most generous one ([PEP 3102](https://peps.python.org/pep-3102/)). Rakudo shipped `MAIN` and `USAGE` in its 2010.06 release ([Rakudo ChangeLog](https://github.com/rakudo/rakudo/blob/main/docs/ChangeLog)); Michele Simionato's plac, which also generates a command line from a function signature, reached PyPI on 2 June 2010 — the same month — out of his own Python Cookbook recipe ([plac](https://pypi.org/project/plac/)), and every later tool in that line, through argh, clize, Fire, Typer and Nim's cligen, descends from the Python side ([cligen](https://github.com/c-blake/cligen)).

Gradual typing is where Perl 6 came closest to acknowledgement and still received none. Jeremy Siek's explainer names Perl 6 alongside Python 3k and ES4 as languages gradual typing should give a foundation to ([Siek](https://jsiek.github.io/home/WhatIsGradualTyping.html)), but the 2006 Siek & Taha paper itself mentions Common Lisp, Dylan, Cecil, Bigloo, Strongtalk and Thatte, not Perl ([Siek & Taha](http://schemeworkshop.org/2006/13-siek.pdf)). Perl 6's model — annotations add checked constraints and an unannotated variable means `Any` — is closer to Common Lisp-style optional typing than to Siek's sound-at-the-boundary gradual typing, so Wikipedia's uncited claim that Raku had gradual typing from the start is true only in the loose sense ([Wikipedia: Gradual typing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradual_typing)). The P0515 survey for C++20's `<=>` is a small but telling data point: it asserts that among major languages only Haskell returns a type from three-way comparison, overlooking Raku's `Order` enum ([P0515R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2017/p0515r0.pdf)), which fits the broader finding that designers' surveys rarely look at Raku at all.

**A full-text search of the proposal archives found no hidden borrowing.** On 7 November 2026 the complete [swift-evolution](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution), [rust-lang/rfcs](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs), [Kotlin KEEP](https://github.com/Kotlin/KEEP), [dotnet/csharplang](https://github.com/dotnet/csharplang) (proposals and design-meeting notes), Scala SIP ([current](https://github.com/scala/improvement-proposals) and [historical](https://github.com/scala/docs.scala-lang)) and [dart-lang/language](https://github.com/dart-lang/language) repositories were searched for Perl 6, Perl6, Raku and Rakudo, along with their GitHub issue and pull-request threads and the JEPs. Only four files matched: the C# chained-comparison proposal, Rust RFC 2909, a Kotlin design note on function colouring, and SE-0363's unused link. The issue threads added the Dart range survey and a handful of Rust comments. All of these are prior art or suggestions not taken, so none changes a verdict, and every other Perl mention in those archives concerns Perl 5 regexes, quoting or tooling. JEP coverage is partial. openjdk.org began refusing requests from this machine after 106 of its 511 pages had arrived, so the missing language, compiler and core-library JEPs were read from Wayback Machine snapshots instead. No JEP read mentions Perl 6 or Raku, and [JEP 111](https://openjdk.org/jeps/111)'s Perl references are to Perl 5.10-era regex constructs. Four places remain unsearched, and much of the design debate happens there: csharplang Discussions, forums.swift.org, contributors.scala-lang.org and internals.rust-lang.org. No primary design notes were found for C# 2.0's `??`, ES2015 shorthand properties, Swift 1.0's pre-open-source choices (`$0`, `\()`, `??`) or Kotlin's `it`. A documented link would most likely surface in the p5p threads behind `//`, `<<~` and Perl 5 chained comparisons, or in the python-3000 threads behind PEP 3102.

| PEG framing of grammars | Ford (POPL 2004), from TDPL/GTDPL | rules in A5 (2002); S05 PEG overview added 5 May 2010 | LPeg (credits SNOBOL4); OMeta (2007); PEP 617 (2020); Pest | **Independent; vocabulary flowed into Perl 6** ([Ford](https://bford.info/pub/lang/peg.pdf); [52d2074](https://github.com/Raku/old-design-docs/commit/52d2074); [LPeg](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/peg.pdf)) |

**Roles are the best-documented common-ancestor case, and the direction is unambiguous.** A12 accepts the Traits paper's argument that classes should not manage both objects and code reuse and lists that paper as its only reference, while departing from it in three ways: roles may hold state (a point Wall defended on perl6-language in December 2003), conflicts are not resolved by renaming or excluding methods, and roles can be applied at runtime as mixins ([A12](https://www.perl.com/pub/2004/04/16/a12.html/); [perl6-language](https://groups.google.com/g/perl.perl6.language/c/kuxrQlzguIo)). The Traits authors then listed Perl 6 roles as an adopter in their 2006 TOPLAS paper, next to Scala ([TOPLAS 2006](https://scg.unibe.ch/archive/papers/Duca06bTOPLASTraits.pdf)). PHP, Java 8 and Groovy cite the Traits papers or the mixin tradition rather than Perl 6; Java's designers described default methods as a limited, stateless form of traits ([FOOL 2011](https://person.dibris.unige.it/zucca-elena/FOOL2011/goetz-abstract.html)). Multiple dispatch is the same story with CLOS in place of Squeak: Julia's 2012 paper places itself with CLOS, Dylan, Cecil and Diesel ([Julia 2012](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1209.5145)), and Stefan Karpinski later said that even CLOS was not a motivation and that Julia's resemblance to Fortress came from shared problems ([julia-dev](https://groups.google.com/g/julia-dev/c/NJ-b_KYZaIA)). Hyper-operators and Julia's dots both mark element-wise application syntactically instead of vectorizing implicitly — A3 made that choice in 2001 with a joke about APL, Julia made it against Chapel in 2017 citing Matlab and NumPy ([A3](https://www.perl.com/pub/2001/10/02/apocalypse3.html/); [Julia blog](https://julialang.org/blog/2017/01/moredots/)) — which is convergence on a principle, not transmission.

**KEEP/UNDO is the strongest unrewarded priority claim in control flow.** A4 specified blocks that run only on success or only on failure in January 2002 ([A4](https://www.perl.com/pub/2002/01/15/apo4.html/)); D shipped the same split as `scope(success)` and `scope(failure)` in February 2006, crediting Alexandrescu's C++ scope-guard macros ([D forum](https://forum.dlang.org/post/du106f$ptt$1@digitaldaemon.com)), and Zig's `errdefer` (2018) grew out of Zig's own error sigils ([Zig](https://andrewkelley.me/post/zig-january-2018-in-review.html)). For an implementer the most useful distinction comes from Perl 5's PPC0004: Raku phasers are compile-time declarations activated by their presence, while `defer` is a statement that takes effect only if execution reaches it ([PPC0004](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0004-defer-block.md)). No counterpart to the ENTER, FIRST, NEXT or LAST phasers was found in any mainstream language.

Graphemes reverse the usual timing. Raku specified grapheme-level characters in 2002 but shipped them in April 2015, while Elixir made `String.length` grapheme-aware in November 2012 and Swift built `Character` on grapheme clusters in 2014, both grounded in UAX #29 with no Perl 6 citation ([Elixir changelog](https://github.com/elixir-lang/elixir/blob/v0.7.2/CHANGELOG.md); [Swift book](https://docs.swift.org/swift-book/documentation/the-swift-programming-language/stringsandcharacters/)); Ilseman later acknowledged Perl 6 only as a peer supporting grapheme-cluster regex semantics ([forum #100](https://forums.swift.org/t/declarative-string-processing-overview/52459/100)). **NFG's distinctive contribution — synthetic codepoints giving O(1) grapheme indexing — remains uncopied** ([MoarVM strings](https://github.com/MoarVM/MoarVM/blob/nqp-mbc/docs/strings.asciidoc)). The `:D`/`:U` smileys likewise arrived after Spec# and Eiffel's attached types, and they are a binder-checked definedness constraint rather than a sum type or a flow analysis, so a claim that Perl 6 had null safety first is not defensible ([04840a3](https://github.com/Raku/old-design-docs/commit/04840a3a6967d9ee641a9a76a1f9ae929b0d0a19)). Grammars show influence flowing into Perl 6's vocabulary after the fact: Ford's 2004 PEG paper does not mention Perl ([Ford](https://bford.info/pub/lang/peg.pdf)), and Larry Wall added the description of Perl 6 patterns as a native PEG implementation to S05 only on 5 May 2010 ([52d2074](https://github.com/Raku/old-design-docs/commit/52d2074)).

**Many famous Perl 6 features were imported, often from Perl 5 CPAN.**

| Perl 6 feature | Source | Source date | Perl 6 date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Junctions | Conway's Quantum::Superpositions, which added `any` and `all` ([Quantum::Superpositions](https://metacpan.org/pod/Quantum::Superpositions)) | CPAN, 9 Aug 2000 | RFC 225, 14 Sep 2000, which points readers to the module ([RFC 225](https://web.archive.org/web/2012/http://dev.perl.org/perl6/rfc/225.html)) |
| given/when | Conway's Switch.pm ([Switch](https://metacpan.org/pod/Switch)) | CPAN, 7 Aug 2000 | Conway's switch RFC 22, 4 Aug 2000; renamed in A4 after Wall worked through options with Conway ([A4](https://www.perl.com/pub/2002/01/15/apo4.html/)) |
| `reduce` | Graham Barr's List::Util ([RFC 76](https://web.archive.org/web/2019/https://raku.org/archive/rfc/76.html)) | before 2000 | RFC 76, 10 Aug 2000 |
| Multimethods | Conway's Class::Multimethods and CLOS ([RFC 256](https://web.archive.org/web/2019/https://raku.org/archive/rfc/256.html)) | before 2000 | RFC 256, 18 Sep 2000 |
| PRE/POST handlers | Conway's Class::Contract ([RFC 271](https://web.archive.org/web/2019/https://raku.org/archive/rfc/271.html)) | before 2000 | RFC 271, 20 Sep 2000 |

The switch module and its RFC appeared within three days of each other from the same author, so they were parallel prototypes rather than a clear sequence. The design documents also openly credit outside languages: Haskell for currying (RFC 23) and laziness (RFC 81) ([RFC 23](https://web.archive.org/web/2019/https://raku.org/archive/rfc/23.html)), APL for hyper-operators and Ada for subtypes, Prolog for the regex cut and for `:=` binding as a kind of unification ([A5](https://www.perl.com/pub/2002/06/04/apo5.html/)), Ruby for unary `*` flattening, Java for interfaces (RFC 265), Unix command lines and Perl 5 I/O layers for colon-pair adverbs, Rx for Supplies ([Advent 2013](https://perl6advent.wordpress.com/2013/12/19/perl-6-supplies-reactive-programming/)), and — per Wall in 2016 — Haskell, Go, Erlang and C# for the concurrency model ([Slashdot 2016](https://developers.slashdot.org/story/16/07/14/1349207/the-slashdot-interview-with-larry-wall)). Three commonly repeated attributions — Icon for gather/take, Eiffel by name for PRE/POST, and Smalltalk — have no primary Perl 6 statement behind them and are third-party glosses.

**What nobody adopted is as informative as what spread.** Junctions have no adopter outside Perl 5 CPAN ports: Python's `any()`/`all()` are eager reductions, and Perl 5.42 explicitly refused junction semantics ([PPC0027](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/ppcs/ppc0027-any-and-all.md)). Hyper-operator syntax, Whatever-currying, NFG synthetics, grammars as subclassable classes, slangs, the ENTER/FIRST/NEXT/LAST phasers and the `:D`/`:U` smileys likewise have no documented counterpart elsewhere, and dynamically scoped `take` remains Raku's alone, since Kotlin's `sequence {}` forbids suspension from called functions by design and Python and JavaScript need `yield from`/`yield*` ([KEEP coroutines](https://github.com/Kotlin/KEEP/blob/master/proposals/coroutines.md)).

## Perl 6 traveled further as people, a VM and a warning

**Perl 6's best-documented influence on another language community ran through Pugs, and it affected Haskell's adoption rather than its design.** Haskell's HOPL III history (2007) names Darcs and Pugs as the open-source projects that drew people to Haskell, recounts that Tang picked Perl 6 as her exercise after reading *Types and Programming Languages*, and records Pugs's growth to about 200 contributors within a year ([HOPL III](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/history.pdf)). Tang adds that Pugs fed back on several GHC extensions and that people who met Haskell through Pugs, such as Edward Kmett, went on to energise its community ([Tang interview](https://andrewshitov.com/2015/05/05/interview-with-audrey-tang/)). Parrot left a cited footprint in VM research rather than in languages: the Lua 5.0 paper (2005) points to Parrot's planned register design as evidence of growing interest in register VMs while claiming Lua 5.0 as the first widely used one ([Lua 5.0](https://www.lua.org/doc/jucs05.pdf)), and the 2008 "Virtual Machine Showdown" paper says the stack-versus-register debate reopened because of Parrot's decision ([VM Showdown](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/10185/1/KC-Virtual-2008.pdf)). No hosted language other than Rakudo ever made Parrot its primary implementation, and active development ended in 2021 ([Wikipedia: Parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parrot_virtual_machine)).

**In other languages' design archives, Perl 6 is mostly a warning.** Guido van Rossum wrote in April 2006 that the Python 3000 process aimed to avoid a repeat of Perl 6 ([Artima](https://www.artima.com/weblogs/viewpost.jsp?thread=155123)) and in 2008 contrasted Python 3 with Perl 6 as a wholly new language and implementation ([Linux Journal](https://www.linuxjournal.com/magazine/interview-guido-van-rossum)); a 2007 April Fools' PEP joked about Perl 6 not yet existing ([PEP 3117](https://peps.python.org/pep-3117/)). Brendan Eich said in 2008 that nobody on ES4 wanted it to resemble Perl 6, and in 2014 granted that Perl 6 was full of good ideas that required a compatibility break ([esdiscuss 2008](https://esdiscuss.org/topic/complexity-tax); [esdiscuss 2014](https://esdiscuss.org/topic/thread-about-es6-on-reddit)). PHP's 2014 vote to skip version 6 cited Perl 6 among its reasons ([PHP RFC: php6](https://wiki.php.net/rfc/php6)); Rust's RFC threads mention it the same way, as the "Perl6/Py3k drama" a Rust 2.0 should avoid and, in the 2017 editions debate, as the model for separating a language (Perl 6) from its implementation (Rakudo) ([rfcs #886](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs/pull/886#issuecomment-272011711); [rfcs #2052](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs/pull/2052#issuecomment-312810251)); and Perl's own proposal process lists the Perl 6 RFC era's habit of proposing features nobody knew how to implement as something to avoid, while Rust modelled its RFC process on Python's PEPs ([PPC motivation](https://github.com/Perl/PPCs/blob/main/docs/motivation.md); [Rust RFC 0002](https://rust-lang.github.io/rfcs/0002-rfc-process.html)). The histories other languages wrote about themselves point elsewhere too: JavaScript's HOPL IV history credits Perl 5 for its regex and array functions and Python for generators, destructuring and comprehensions, and never mentions Perl 6 ([HOPL JS](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/brendan-eich/js-hopl.pdf)); Chris Lattner's list of Swift influences omits Perl entirely ([nondot.org](https://nondot.org/sabre/)). As of November 2026 Wikidata records no language as influenced by Raku, Raku's own Wikipedia "Influenced" entries rest on a single 2010 Tang talk, and Haskell's listing of Raku cites a Perl 6 wiki glossary page ([Wikidata Q2052676](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q2052676); [Wikipedia: Raku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raku_(programming_language)); [Wikipedia: Haskell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskell)). Larry Wall himself presented Perl 6 as a borrower, saying in 2016 that other languages had stolen Perl 5's regexes and that any language borrowing enough of Perl 6 to be consistent would simply have reimplemented it ([Slashdot 2016](https://developers.slashdot.org/story/16/07/14/1349207/the-slashdot-interview-with-larry-wall)).

## Conclusion

The evidence separates what Perl 6 got right from what it transmitted. Its specification picked several answers the industry later converged on, five to twenty years early — the closing-delimiter heredoc rule and defined-or in 2001, success/failure scope blocks, `/x` by default and grammars as classes in 2002, named-only parameters in 2003 — but almost none of that judgment turned into citations, because ideas travel with shippable implementations and with the languages designers already use. What did cross the boundary was **small, separable syntax or semantics that a host language could adopt without buying into the rest**: an arrow token, a string-increment rule, a regex whitespace default, a CPAN object system. The features that demand whole-language commitment — junction autothreading, hyper and meta-operators, Whatever-currying, NFG, grammar subclassing, slangs — have not moved at all, and their non-adoption measures how much of Raku is architecture rather than syntax.

Two practical lessons follow for a Raku implementer. Priority claims need the right date: the Apocalypse establishes the idea, but Pugs (2005) and Rakudo (2009–2015) decide whether anyone outside could have used it, and late-specified features such as `:D`, `sub MAIN`, NFG and Supplies were contemporaries of their analogues, not ancestors. And Perl 5's smartmatch is the case study in borrowing too early — it froze a 2002–2007 snapshot of a design Raku then revised, and spent eighteen years unable to fix or remove it. The pieces of Raku now being cited in PHP, Ruby and Swift discussions — range endpoints, rational defaults, regex ergonomics — are exactly the settled, separable ones, which suggests that stable and well-documented semantics for such pieces, rather than the ambition of the whole design, are what other languages will continue to pick up.
